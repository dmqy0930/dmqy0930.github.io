Transcription–Replication Conflicts as Dynamic Regulators of Chromatin States: From Genome Instability to Epigenetic Memory

Authors: [Author 1], [Author 2], [Corresponding Author]

Correspondence: [email]

Abstract

Transcription and DNA replication are the two most fundamental DNA-templated processes, and both must operate on the same genomic template within the crowded nuclear environment. Their spatial and temporal overlap generates transcription–replication conflicts (TRCs), a major endogenous source of replication stress, R-loop accumulation, DNA damage and genome instability. Many previous work has established TRCs as pathological collisions that threaten genome stability. However, genome-wide analyses increasingly show that TRCs are not randomly distributed: their occurrence is shaped by replication timing, origin selection, transcriptional architecture, chromatin accessibility, R-loop propensity and three-dimensional genome organization. Conversely, unresolved TRCs elicit DNA damage responses and chromatin remodelling that can persist beyond the acute damage, raising the possibility that TRCs not only damage the genome but also reshape chromatin states and, potentially, influence cell identity. Here, we propose an integrative framework in which TRCs are governed by three interactive layers: chromatin-determined TRC susceptibility; cellular buffering mechanisms that prevent, sense and resolve conflicts; and TRC-induced chromatin remodelling that may feed back on transcriptional programmes and cell fate. We discuss how imbalances in this TRC–chromatin equilibrium contribute to cancer evolution, aging and developmental transitions, and we outline the experimental and computational strategies needed to test whether TRCs can establish stable epigenetic memory.

Keywords

transcription–replication conflict; R-loop; replication stress; replication timing; chromatin remodelling; epigenetic memory; genome instability; cancer evolution; ageing; cell identity

Introduction

The eukaryotic genome is not a passive store of genetic information but a crowded platform, on which DNA replication, RNA transcription, DNA repair and chromatin remodelling operate simultaneously on the same template. Each of these processes is highly dynamic: replication forks traverse the genome at kilobases per minute, RNA polymerase II (Pol II) tracks along transcribed units at variable speeds, and repair factors scan for lesions among nucleosomes and regulatory complexes. Because they share the DNA template, these machineries must be coordinated in space and time; when coordination fails, the physical encounter between a replication fork and the transcription apparatus creates a transcription–replication conflict (TRC) [1–2].

TRCs can be classified by the direction of movement of the two machineries. In head-on conflicts, the replication fork approaches the polymerase from the opposite direction; in co-directional conflicts, the fork moves in the same direction as transcription. Early work, largely in yeast and bacteria, established that both types of conflict slow fork progression, destabilize the replisome and promote DNA breaks and rearrangements, with head-on collisions generally considered the more damaging [2–3]. In mammalian genomes, however, the frequent proximity of replication origins to active promoters means that co-directional conflicts may be both frequent and physiologically relevant [4]. Functional genomics and single-molecule studies have since shown that conflict orientation determines not only the severity of damage but also the type of DNA damage response engaged [4].

The consequences of TRCs are now well documented. Conflict-driven fork stalling exposes single-stranded DNA, promotes the formation of RNA–DNA hybrids (R-loops), and can lead to fork collapse, double-strand breaks and chromosomal rearrangements [5–7]. These lesions are potent sources of endogenous mutation and structural variation, and TRCs have been linked to common fragile site instability, oncogene-induced replication stress and tumour evolution [3,8–9]. Accordingly, most TRC research to date has framed conflicts as accidental, pathological collisions.

Three observations argue that the “accidental collision” view is incomplete. First, TRCs do not occur uniformly across the genome. Conflict-prone regions cluster at specific loci: highly transcribed genes, long genes, CpG-island promoters, super-enhancers, early-replicating fragile sites and certain repeat families [2–4,10]. Second, the probability of conflict at any given locus depends on the local chromatin environment—nucleosome occupancy, histone modifications, replication timing and three-dimensional positioning—meaning that the epigenetic landscape actively determines where conflicts can arise. Third, R-loops, the central nucleic-acid intermediate of TRCs, are not merely toxic by-products; they are abundant, developmentally regulated and associated with characteristic epigenomic signatures, consistent with regulatory as well as pathological functions [10–12].

These findings raise a fundamental question that frames this review: do TRCs sit upstream of chromatin regulation as well as downstream of it? In other words, can the chromatin state determine TRC susceptibility (a bottom-up relationship), while unresolved TRCs in turn remodel chromatin and influence gene expression and cell fate (a top-down relationship)? The existence of such bidirectional regulation would reposition TRCs from stochastic accidents to an information-bearing layer that connects genome stress with epigenetic state.

We organize the current evidence into a three-layer framework (Fig. 1):

  • Layer 1 — Chromatin determines TRC vulnerability. Replication timing, origin selection, transcriptional architecture, R-loop propensity and three-dimensional genome folding define genomic “TRC hotspots”.
  • Layer 2 — Cellular buffering maintains TRC homeostasis. Cells prevent, sense and resolve conflicts through dedicated molecular systems; disease arises when TRC burden exceeds buffering capacity.
  • Layer 3 — TRCs reshape chromatin. Persistent or recurrent TRCs trigger DNA-damage-dependent and R-loop-dependent chromatin remodelling that may produce adaptive chromatin states with consequences for gene expression, cell identity and disease.

What this review adds is that previous reviews have covered TRCs as products of transcription and replication [1–2] and R-loops as chromatin regulators with both regulatory and pathological functions [10–12], but each has treated one direction of the relationship. This review differs in three respects. First, it makes the bidirectional coupling between chromatin and TRCs the organizing principle: chromatin state determines where conflicts arise, and unresolved conflicts can in turn remodel chromatin. Second, it turns the burden-versus-capacity intuition into an explicit three-level buffering framework with defined prevention, sensing and resolution components and a testable threshold. Third, it provides a rubric (Box 1) for judging when TRC-induced chromatin change qualifies as epigenetic memory, together with experimental and computational designs that could discriminate the three models described later in this review. We therefore frame this review not as a summary of conflict pathology but as a proposal for testing whether TRCs are information-bearing events.

Throughout, we distinguish evidence from hypothesis. The claim that TRCs can act as “epigenetic signals” that create heritable memory is currently a proposal supported by partial evidence; we treat it as such and define the criteria that would be required to substantiate it (Box 1).

Chromatin landscapes determine the spatial and temporal vulnerability of TRCs

TRC hotspots: conflict is locus-specific, not stochastic

If TRCs were purely stochastic collisions, their genomic distribution would simply mirror transcription and replication density. Instead, genome-wide mapping of replication stress markers, R-loops and conflict-associated damage consistently identifies discrete hotspots that cannot be explained by transcription intensity alone [2,10]. For example, common fragile sites are enriched at very long, actively transcribed genes that replicate late or asynchronously, where a single fork must traverse a large transcription unit within a limited time window [3]. Conversely, early-replicating fragile sites (ERFSs) are enriched in highly transcribed, GC-rich, open-chromatin regions that fire early in S phase, showing that early replication does not exempt a locus from conflict [13]. The emerging picture is that TRC susceptibility is a function of several interacting parameters: when a locus replicates, where its origins fire, how heavily it is transcribed, whether transcription generates R-loops, and how the locus is positioned in the nucleus.

Replication timing and origin selection set the temporal window of conflict

Replication in mammalian cells proceeds according to a developmentally regulated, cell-type-specific timing programme in which megabase-scale domains replicate at characteristic times during S phase [14–17]. In general, actively transcribed genes and open chromatin replicate early, whereas heterochromatic and nuclear-periphery regions replicate late [14,16]. Replication timing is not a passive readout of expression: differentiation is accompanied by large-scale switching of replication domains, and these switches are coordinated with transcriptional and three-dimensional genome reorganization [15,17]. Importantly, topologically associating domains (TADs) are stable units of replication-timing regulation, and early/late replication correlates with active/inactive chromatin compartments defined by Hi-C [18–19]. Thus, the replication programme is, to a large extent, a chromatin-level schedule that has evolved to minimize—but cannot eliminate—encounters with the transcription machinery [20].

Origin selection is equally important. Unlike budding yeast, metazoan origins are not defined by a simple consensus sequence; they are flexible, partially stochastic and strongly biased toward open chromatin, CpG islands, promoters and G-quadruplex-forming motifs [21–23]. Because origins frequently fire near transcription start sites, the first few kilobases of transcribed units are zones of inherently high TRC risk, and the directionality of forks emanating from promoter-proximal origins means that many conflicts in mammals are co-directional [4,23]. Repli-seq and OK-seq have made it possible to map replication timing and fork direction genome-wide, revealing that fork direction is often oriented away from highly transcribed genes—an apparent evolutionary adaptation that nevertheless leaves some loci, particularly long genes and loci with convergent transcription, in a head-on configuration [16,24].

Origin choice is itself influenced by the chromatin states that define TRC hotspots, creating a circular relationship that the field has only begun to explore. Because metazoan origins are preferentially licensed and fired at open, transcriptionally permissive regions, many lie close to or within transcribed units [21–23]. The same chromatin features that promote transcription therefore also promote the initiation events that can collide with it, and the degree of overlap between origin zones and transcription units varies across cell types and developmental states [23]. Whether this overlap is selected for a regulatory function or is an unavoidable cost of organizing origins in accessible chromatin is unresolved, and it represents a specific gap that a quantitative susceptibility model (see Future perspectives) could address.

Active chromatin and promoter-proximal pausing create transcription-associated vulnerability

Transcription itself is the source of TRCs, and the regulatory architecture of transcription determines where conflicts concentrate. Promoter-proximal pausing is a pervasive, rate-limiting step of Pol II transcription in metazoans: after initiation, Pol II stalls ~30–60 nucleotides downstream of the transcription start site, held by NELF and DSIF, before P-TEFb releases it into productive elongation [25–26]. PRO-seq has provided precise maps of engaged polymerases and revealed that pausing is a major determinant of the transcriptional output of promoters [27]. Paused polymerases are topological obstacles: a replication fork approaching a paused Pol II complex must displace a stable, sequence-specifically engaged polymerase, and the stalled transcription bubble is a favourable substrate for R-loop formation [4,28]. Consistent with this, R-loops form preferentially at CpG-island promoters that are enriched for paused Pol II, and their positions correlate with promoter-proximal chromatin architecture [28–29].

Pausing therefore has two opposing effects on TRC susceptibility. By holding Pol II at a defined position near the transcription start site, pausing increases the probability that a fork encounters a stable, sequence-specifically engaged polymerase at the very region where origins are most often located; yet pausing also poises genes for rapid induction and limits the number of polymerases traversing the gene body at any moment [25–26]. The net contribution of pausing to conflict burden is therefore locus- and cell-type-specific, and it can only be assessed by combining polymerase-occupancy maps with fork-direction and replication-timing data at the same loci [24,27]. This interdependence is one reason why transcriptional output alone is a poor predictor of TRC hotspots, as discussed above.

Super-enhancers—large, densely occupied enhancer clusters that drive cell-identity genes—represent an extreme case. Genome-wide R-loop maps show elevated R-loop levels at enhancer-associated loci [30], and these clusters are characterized by exceptionally high Pol II loading and abundant enhancer RNA synthesis [31]. Their very function—sustaining high transcription of lineage-defining genes—makes them structurally prone to conflicts, a property that may be relevant to their vulnerability to replication stress in cancer [32–33]. More generally, an increase in global transcription activity, as occurs upon oncogene activation, is sufficient to induce replication stress and DNA damage even without changes in the replication programme, directly linking transcriptional load to conflict burden [32].

R-loops, sequence determinants and three-dimensional genome organization

R-loops—three-stranded structures in which nascent RNA invades the template DNA strand, leaving the non-template strand single-stranded—are both a cause and a consequence of TRCs [10–12]. Their formation is strongly influenced by sequence and chromatin features: G-rich, GC-skewed regions at promoters and terminators, unmethylated CpG islands, and gene bodies enriched in H3K36me3 are hotspots of R-loop formation [28,30,34]. R-loop-positive promoters and terminators display distinctive epigenetic signatures, including H3K4 methylation at promoters and H3K36me3 at terminators, indicating that R-loop-prone transcription is coupled to specific chromatin states rather than being a uniformly random process [30]. At the same time, R-loops can themselves recruit or exclude chromatin regulators, a theme developed later in this review.

Three-dimensional genome organization adds a further layer. Replication and transcription occur in spatially organized “factories”, and the nuclear positioning of a locus—its compartment (A/B), its association with the nuclear periphery or nucleolus, and its loop architecture—correlates with both replication timing and transcriptional activity [17–19]. Hi-C studies show that early-replicating, active compartments are enriched in transcription and replication components, raising the possibility that spatial co-compartmentalization increases, rather than decreases, the probability of encounter between the two machineries [19]. The net effect of 3D organization on TRC frequency is therefore context-dependent: spatial separation may protect some loci, while the clustering of active loci into transcription/replication factories may concentrate conflict risk in a subset of nuclear territories.

An important technical caveat follows from how these features are detected. Replication-stress readouts report the aftermath of fork encounters, whereas R-loop maps (DRIP-seq, R-ChIP) report the nucleic-acid intermediate directly and are enriched at promoter-proximal and termination zones [28,30,34]. Fork-direction data (OK-seq) add a further dimension by revealing which transcribed units are traversed head-on rather than co-directionally [16,24]. Because each assay captures a different stage of conflict, integrating orthogonal readouts at the same locus is necessary to distinguish a genuine TRC hotspot from a site that accumulates R-loops or repair factors for unrelated reasons; the strategies for such integration are discussed in the Future perspectives section.

TRC susceptibility is therefore a chromatin-encoded property. The main determinants—replication timing, origin location, transcriptional load, pausing, R-loop propensity, sequence features and 3D folding—interact to define a genome-wide landscape of TRC hotspots (Fig. 1, left). A key unresolved question is how these parameters are integrated: no current model quantitatively predicts TRC probability from epigenomic data, and single-cell variability in replication timing and transcription is largely unexplored in this context.

Cellular buffering: prevention, sensing and resolution of TRCs

The concept of TRC buffering capacity and a threshold model

A remarkable feature of normal cells is that TRCs occur constantly yet rarely produce overt genome instability. This is because evolution has equipped cells with a multi-layered buffering system that operates at three levels: prevention (reducing the probability that a conflict forms), sensing (detecting stalled forks and aberrant nucleic-acid structures), and resolution (removing toxic intermediates and restoring replication) [1–2,8]. We propose that the relevant physiological variable is not the absolute number of TRCs but the balance between TRC burden and buffering capacity. When burden stays below capacity, conflicts are resolved with little consequence; when burden exceeds capacity—through oncogenic transcription, loss of repair factors, or developmental windows of transcriptional upheaval—replication catastrophe, genome instability and cell-fate change ensue [8–9,33,35]. This threshold model is useful conceptually, but it is important to state clearly that a quantitative, experimentally defined threshold does not yet exist; defining it is one of the central challenges for the field.

To make this balance testable, both sides of the equation can be proxied with existing readouts. TRC burden can be estimated from R-loop abundance (DRIP-seq or R-ChIP), fork-stall frequency, γH2AX or 53BP1 foci and CHK1 phosphorylation; buffering capacity can be estimated from the expression or activity of resolution and protection factors (RNase H1/H2, SETX, FANCM and the FA pathway), fork-reversal and restart competence, and the efficiency of the replication-stress response. We emphasize that the threshold itself remains a conceptual framework rather than a measured constant; assigning quantitative thresholds to these proxies, and validating them in defined experimental systems, is a central challenge for the field.

Prevention: limiting conflict formation

The first line of defence operates before a conflict can occur, and it is largely chromatin-based.

Temporal separation via the replication timing programme. The replication programme schedules the duplication of active, early-replicating domains at times and rates that accommodate ongoing transcription [14,17]. Housekeeping and cell-identity genes are generally transcribed throughout the cell cycle, so temporal separation alone cannot eliminate conflicts; it can only shift their probability [3,20].

Spatial separation via nuclear organization. Replication and transcription are spatially organized, and the localization of a locus within the nucleus influences its exposure to each machinery [17,19]. Whether spatial segregation protects specific loci or concentrates conflict risk in shared factories remains to be determined experimentally.

Evolutionary evidence underscores the cost of conflict direction: in bacteria, genomes are organized so that replication and transcription are predominantly co-oriented, and forced head-on conflicts destabilize the genome—suggesting that the relative direction of the two machineries is itself a buffering parameter that cells optimize where possible [36].

Transcriptional regulation at the level of Pol II. Promoter-proximal pausing, elongation control and termination modulate the number and stability of polymerase complexes that a replication fork may encounter [25–26]. Pausing can be protective when it poises genes for rapid induction, but a stably paused polymerase is itself a formidable barrier; the balance between these effects is likely locus-specific [4,25].

Chromatin compaction and remodelling. Heterochromatin at late-replicating, transcriptionally silent regions reduces both transcription and origin activity, effectively lowering conflict probability. Conversely, the SWI/SNF family of ATP-dependent chromatin remodellers promotes R-loop resolution at conflict-prone loci, illustrating that chromatin state is not merely a passive determinant of conflict risk but an active component of the buffering system [37].

Sensing: detecting stalled forks and R-loops

When prevention fails, cells must detect the conflict rapidly and signal downstream effectors.

The ATR–CHK1 replication stress response. Fork stalling exposes single-stranded DNA that is rapidly coated by RPA, leading to recruitment of ATR–ATRIP and activation of CHK1 [8,38]. ATR is the central kinase of the replication stress response: it stabilizes forks, suppresses origin firing, enforces the S–G2 checkpoint and coordinates repair [38]. The essentiality of ATR in proliferating cells reflects the constant, endogenous burden of replication stress, of which TRCs are a major component [8,38].

R-loop sensors. Because R-loops are the key nucleic-acid intermediate of TRCs, cells have evolved dedicated R-loop recognition systems [10–11]. RPA itself binds R-loops, and RPA directly stimulates RNase H1 activity on RNA–DNA hybrids, establishing RPA as a shared sensor of replication stress and R-loops [11,39]. The Fanconi anaemia (FA) pathway is a second major sensor: FANCM, a DNA translocase, recognizes replication-fork and R-loop structures, and FANCD2 monoubiquitination protects stalled forks and suppresses R-loop-associated damage [40–41]. BRCA1 and senataxin (SETX) form complexes at transcriptional pause sites, where they are required for R-loop-driven DNA damage repair, connecting transcription-associated R-loop sensing to the homologous-recombination machinery [42]. Transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair factors have also been implicated in R-loop biology, although their engagement can promote R-loop-induced genome instability rather than protect against it [43].

The sensing layer is also connected to chromatin remodelling at stalled forks. When RPA-coated single-stranded DNA accumulates, ATR signalling restrains late-origin firing and enforces the S–G2 checkpoint, buying time for resolution and preventing the conversion of a local stall into a genome-wide catastrophe [8,38]. The FA pathway couples these events to the transcription apparatus: FANCM recognizes both fork and R-loop structures, and FANCD2 monoubiquitination stabilizes stalled forks while suppressing R-loop-associated damage [40–41]. BRCA1 and SETX act at transcriptional pause sites to connect R-loop sensing to homologous-recombination repair [42], illustrating that the sensing machinery is itself positioned at the chromatin features that define TRC-prone loci.

Resolution: eliminating toxic intermediates and restoring forks

Once a conflict or an R-loop is detected, dedicated resolution systems remove the offending structures.

RNase H1 and RNase H2 specifically degrade the RNA moiety of RNA–DNA hybrids and are the primary enzymes for R-loop removal; their inactivation causes R-loop accumulation, replication stress and genome instability [44–45], and germline mutations in RNase H2 underlie the autoinflammatory disease Aicardi–Goutières syndrome (see the section on ageing). Overexpression of RNase H is the standard experimental tool for suppressing R-loops, underscoring their centrality to TRC pathology [44,46].

Helicases and RNA-processing factors. Senataxin (SETX) unwinds RNA–DNA hybrids at transcriptional pause sites and terminators, promoting XRN2-dependent termination and preventing R-loop-induced damage [46–47]. RECQ-family helicases and DDX-family RNA helicases act on R-loop structures and stalled forks, and their loss recapitulates transcription-associated instability [2,11]. Co-transcriptional processing and efficient termination are themselves protective: defects in 3’-end processing and termination collapse R-loops into pathological structures [47].

Fork remodelling and restart. Stalled forks are not passive victims of conflict; they are actively remodelled into reversed forks that stabilize the replication apparatus and provide a platform for repair [48–49]. Fork reversal is promoted by RAD51, SMARCAL1, ZRANB3 and HLTF, and reversed forks must be restarted—for example by RECQ1—once the obstruction is cleared; dysregulation of this process converts a reversible stall into a double-strand break [48–49]. The FA pathway and BRCA1/2 protect nascent DNA at reversed forks, coupling conflict resolution to the fidelity of the replication programme [40,42].

The reversal and restart cycle is intrinsically bidirectional and error-prone. Reversed forks must be protected from nucleolytic degradation by BRCA1/2 and FA-pathway factors, and restart by RECQ1 must be coordinated with lesion clearance, otherwise the reversed fork becomes a substrate for double-strand break formation rather than a protective structure [48–49]. The balance between fork protection and fork processing therefore determines whether a TRC-associated stall is resolved silently or converted into a heritable rearrangement, a distinction that is central to the threshold framework described above.

Why do some TRCs escape buffering?

If the buffering system is so elaborate, why do conflicts still cause disease? Three answers emerge from the literature. First, transcription is biologically non-negotiable: developmental, immune and oncogenic transcription programmes must run at high intensity for extended periods, and any increase in transcriptional load—without any change in repair capacity—is sufficient to induce replication stress [32,35]. Second, some genomic regions are intrinsically vulnerable: very long genes, ERFSs, super-enhancers and repeat-rich loci present structural challenges that no amount of buffering can fully overcome [3,13,33]. Third, buffering capacity itself is genetically constrained: loss of BRCA1/2, FA genes or chromatin modifiers such as SETD2 lowers the threshold at which TRC burden becomes damaging, explaining why such lesions are selected in cancer [42,50]. The threshold model therefore predicts that disease arises at the intersection of elevated burden and reduced capacity, a prediction that is testable with current technologies (see Future perspectives).

TRC homeostasis is maintained by a tripartite buffering system whose components are themselves chromatin-associated and dynamically recruited (Fig. 1, middle). The most urgent mechanistic gaps concern how prevention factors are recruited to predicted TRC hotspots before conflict occurs, and how buffering capacity is calibrated to locus-specific conflict load.

TRCs reshape chromatin: from DNA damage response to epigenetic memory

The most innovative—and least established—part of the framework is that TRCs are not only products of the chromatin environment but also architects of it.

Acute chromatin responses to TRC-associated damage

DNA damage is a potent chromatin-remodelling signal. Double-strand breaks trigger the rapid phosphorylation of H2AX (γH2AX), which nucleates a cascade of ubiquitin and SUMO signalling that recruits repair factors and remodels the local nucleosome landscape [51–54]. Break-proximal chromatin undergoes extensive changes in histone modifications—including acetylation, methylation and ubiquitination—that are required for both repair and checkpoint signalling [52,54–55]. A particularly relevant consequence is the local silencing of transcription: ATM-dependent chromatin changes at break sites repress Pol II transcription in cis, both reducing further transcription-replication interference and preventing the production of aberrant transcripts from damaged templates [51]. This acute response is conceptually important because it demonstrates a direct, chromatin-mediated route by which a TRC-derived lesion can alter gene expression in its immediate vicinity.

Several features of this acute response make it relevant to the long-term question posed by this review. First, the chromatin changes are locally restricted and cis-acting: ATM-dependent silencing operates in the immediate vicinity of the break rather than globally [51]. Second, they are reversible in principle, because repair completion and checkpoint recovery restore transcription and chromatin organization [52–55]. Third, the same nucleosome and histone-modification machinery is shared with replication-coupled chromatin assembly, so the boundary between a transient repair-associated state and a propagated epigenetic state is set by how marks are restored after the fork passes [52,55]. These parallels frame the persistence criteria in Box 1 and motivate the longitudinal experiments outlined in the Future perspectives section.

R-loops as chromatin-regulatory intermediates

Independent of DNA breaks, R-loops themselves are emerging as bona fide chromatin-regulatory structures [11–12,56–57]. Genome-wide analyses show that R-loop-positive promoters and terminators carry distinct histone-modification signatures, and that R-loops can recruit or exclude specific chromatin regulators: they promote H3K4 methylation at promoters and associate with H3K36me3 at gene bodies [30], and can enhance Polycomb repression at developmental genes [47,56]. R-loops also influence promoter-proximal nucleosome positioning and chromatin architecture in ways that affect cellular differentiation [29]. Strikingly, persistent R-loops are associated with H3S10 phosphorylation and chromatin condensation, linking co-transcriptional hybrid formation to higher-order chromatin compaction [58]. These observations, together with the demonstration that R-loop levels are dynamically coupled to Pol II pausing at promoters [59], support the view that R-loops—the most abundant TRC intermediate—function as local chromatin-modifying platforms whose regulatory output depends on whether they are resolved or persist [11–12,57].

Does TRC-induced chromatin change persist? The question of epigenetic memory

The central hypothesis of this review is that recurrent or unresolved TRCs could leave a lasting imprint on chromatin—a form of stress-adapted state that alters subsequent gene expression and cell behaviour. Before evaluating this hypothesis, it is necessary to define what “epigenetic memory” would mean in this context. We propose the following criteria (Box 1):

  1. Persistence: the chromatin change outlasts the inducing TRC (e.g., after removal of the stress or passage through cell division).
  2. Function: the change measurably affects transcription or other chromatin-templated processes.
  3. Self-propagation: the state is maintained through DNA replication and mitosis independently of the original trigger, via the inheritance of histone modifications, histone variants, or feedback loops.
  4. Reversibility: the state can be erased or reset under defined conditions, distinguishing epigenetic memory from permanent genetic change.

How much evidence currently exists for each criterion? Criterion 2 is well supported: TRC-associated lesions and R-loops demonstrably alter transcription locally [29,51,58]. Criterion 1 has partial support: DDR-induced chromatin modifications can persist for hours to days, and replication-stress-associated chromatin states have been observed after stress removal, but systematic longitudinal studies are lacking [52,55]. Criterion 3 is essentially untested for TRCs specifically. Chromatin replication itself poses a formidable barrier to histone-mark inheritance, as nucleosomes are disrupted at the fork and parental marks are diluted among newly deposited histones; nevertheless, defined epigenetic states can be stably propagated in model systems, and histone-based memory can be inherited through many cell divisions when counteracting erasers are inactivated [20,60–62]. Criterion 4 is experimentally addressable but has not been examined for TRC-induced states.

It is therefore premature to state that TRCs “establish” epigenetic memory. The evidence justifies a weaker but still significant claim: TRCs can induce persistent local chromatin remodelling that may influence transcription and cell behaviour; whether such changes constitute self-propagating epigenetic memory remains an open question [10,56].

Proposed models: transient adaptation, memory, and selection

We propose three non-exclusive models for the long-term consequences of TRC-induced chromatin change:

Model A — Transient chromatin adaptation. Repeated TRCs at a hotspot recruit buffering factors and modify local chromatin (e.g., by increasing nucleosome turnover or depositing specific marks), temporarily raising the local buffering capacity without creating heritable change. This model is consistent with the observation that DDR and R-loop regulators are dynamically recruited to conflict sites and with the general principle that chromatin is a responsive, rather than static, scaffold [52,55].

Model B — Stable epigenetic memory. Under conditions in which erasers are limiting or feedback loops are engaged, TRC-induced chromatin states self-propagate through cell divisions and alter the transcriptional identity of the locus or the cell. Direct evidence is lacking, and the strong constraints on histone-mark inheritance across the replication fork make this the most demanding model [20,61–62].

Model C — Darwinian selection of TRC-adapted states. In heterogeneous cell populations (particularly cancer), cells that acquire chromatin states or genetic alterations that increase TRC tolerance are selected, generating an apparent “memory” at the population level even if individual cells do not stably inherit chromatin marks. This model does not require epigenetic heritability and is consistent with the rapid evolution of replication-stress tolerance in tumours [33,63–64].

Distinguishing these models will require lineage-tracing experiments combined with multi-omics readouts of chromatin state, transcription and replication stress across many cell divisions (Fig. 3; see Future perspectives).

TRC-associated DNA damage and R-loops are therefore established inducers of local chromatin remodelling. Whether such remodelling becomes functionally consequential and heritable—the “epigenetic signal” hypothesis—remains unproven, and we explicitly frame it as a testable proposal rather than an established fact (Fig. 1, right; Box 1).

The core hypothesis revisited: supporting evidence, limitations and recommended formulations

The proposition that TRCs act as epigenetic signals is the conceptual core of this review, so we now state precisely what the current literature does and does not support.

Supporting evidence. Three independent bodies of evidence converge on the plausibility of TRC-driven chromatin regulation. First, R-loops, the defining intermediate of TRCs, are established regulators of chromatin: they shape promoter-proximal nucleosome architecture and cellular differentiation [29], associate with characteristic histone-modification signatures at promoters and terminators [30,56], modulate Polycomb-mediated repression and chromatin condensation [47,58], and are dynamically coupled to Pol II pausing [59]. Second, the DNA damage responses triggered by unresolved TRCs produce local chromatin changes—including ATM-dependent transcriptional silencing—that can outlast the initiating lesion [51–52,55]. Third, chromatin replication provides a mechanistic route by which histone-associated information can be transmitted to daughter cells, even though the fork is also the site at which epigenetic information is diluted and must be restored [20,60–62]; precedents in yeast demonstrate that stress-induced chromatin states can be self-propagating when erasing activities are limited [61–62].

Current limitations. Direct evidence that TRCs specifically—as opposed to DNA damage or R-loop accumulation in general—produce heritable chromatin states is lacking. All existing data are population averages: no study has tracked a defined TRC through cell division at single-locus, single-cell resolution. Interpretation is further complicated by three confounds: (i) low-level ongoing conflict may maintain a chromatin change without true self-propagation; (ii) apparent persistence may reflect clonal selection of pre-existing tolerant cells rather than epigenetic inheritance (Model C); and (iii) histone-mark propagation across the fork is inefficient, and transcription-coupled processes can actively erase marks, so any TRC-derived memory would require dedicated reinforcement mechanisms [20,61]. Human genetics provides supporting but indirect evidence: loss of the R-loop-resolving helicase SETX causes the neurodegenerative disorder ataxia with oculomotor apraxia type 2, demonstrating that failed R-loop resolution has cell-fate consequences, but not that TRC-induced chromatin states are heritable [65].

Recommended formulation. On the basis of this evidence, the following wording is appropriate for the Abstract and concluding sections:

“TRCs can induce persistent local chromatin remodelling that may influence transcription and cell behaviour; whether such changes constitute self-propagating epigenetic memory remains an open question.”

Minimal experimental roadmap. Three experiments would move this question from correlation to causation: (1) an inducible, locus-specific TRC system—for example, transcription driven through a replication origin that can be switched on and off—followed by multi-generation CUT&Tag, PRO-seq and Repli-seq profiling after removal of the inducer; (2) lineage-resolved single-cell tracking to distinguish epigenetic inheritance from ongoing stress and from clonal selection; and (3) perturbation of R-loop metabolism (RNase H overexpression or depletion; SETX depletion) to test whether any persistent state is R-loop-dependent [44–45,59,65]. Together with the multi-omics framework in the Future perspectives section, these experiments would directly discriminate Model B from Models A and C.

Disease implications: the TRC–chromatin equilibrium in cell fate

A general framework: disease as a shift in the TRC–chromatin equilibrium

The buffering framework developed above predicts that disease arises when the balance between TRC burden and buffering capacity is disturbed, and that the resulting chromatin changes feed back on transcriptional programmes. This perspective unifies several disease contexts that are usually discussed separately: cancer, ageing and developmental disorders can all be viewed as states in which the TRC–chromatin equilibrium is shifted, with consequences for cell identity [33,63,66].

Cancer: oncogenic transcription, buffering collapse and chromatin adaptation

Cancer is the best-studied context in which TRC burden exceeds buffering capacity. Two complementary mechanisms drive this imbalance. First, oncogene activation increases the generation of TRCs: oncogenes such as MYC amplify both transcription and replication, and the resulting transcriptional overload is sufficient to induce replication stress and DNA damage [32,67–68]. Second, tumour suppressors that safeguard buffering capacity are frequently lost: BRCA1/2 deficiency compromises fork protection and R-loop resolution, FA-pathway defects permit R-loop-associated damage, and loss of the H3K36me3 methyltransferase SETD2 impairs homologous-recombination repair and elevates replication stress [40,42,50]. The convergence of elevated burden and reduced capacity explains why replication stress is an early, pervasive feature of premalignant lesions and why it fuels the mutational and rearrangement processes that drive tumour evolution [33,63,67].

Importantly, the relationship between TRCs and cancer is not limited to damage. The chromatin-remodelling consequences of chronic replication stress may contribute to the aberrant transcriptional programmes of tumour cells—an “adaptive” layer that, together with genetic selection, stabilizes malignant states [33,64]. This idea remains speculative in its strong form, but it makes concrete predictions: TRC hotspots in cancer cells should coincide with loci of chromatin-state change, and interventions that alter the balance (for example, increasing conflict burden therapeutically) should destabilize tumour-adapted chromatin states [69].

The temporal order of these events matters for interpreting cancer genomes. Replication stress is now recognized as an early, pre-malignant event: oncogene activation can induce DNA damage and cellular senescence barriers before overt transformation [67], and the replication-stress landscape of premalignant lesions is consistent with an early role in tumour evolution [33,63]. Once transformation is underway, the selective pressure to tolerate elevated conflict burden shapes the mutational and copy-number landscape of evolving tumours [33,63–64].

Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA): a special case. ecDNAs are circular, chromatinized amplicons that carry oncogenes in many aggressive tumours [70]. They lack centromeres and segregate non-mendelianly, yet they are transcribed at exceptionally high levels and are organized into intermolecular hubs that further amplify oncogene expression [70–71]. Because ecDNAs are both highly transcribed and replicated, they are predicted—and have recently been shown—to be sites of intense transcription–replication conflict; notably, enhancing TRCs selectively eliminates ecDNA-positive cancer cells, identifying the conflict axis as a therapeutically exploitable vulnerability of ecDNA-driven tumours [69]. ecDNAs therefore provide a natural experiment for the TRC–chromatin framework: their open, highly transcribed chromatin makes them permanent TRC hotspots, and their epigenetic plasticity may underlie both oncogene overexpression and tolerance to replication stress.

Ageing: accumulated TRCs and the deterioration of genome and epigenome

Replication stress has long been proposed to contribute to ageing, and the underlying logic is consistent with the buffering framework: as cells age, both TRC burden (through accumulated DNA lesions, transcription-associated stress and altered replication) and buffering capacity (through declining repair and fork-protection efficiency) move in the wrong direction [66,72]. Senescent cells mount a chronic DNA damage response, and DNA-damage signalling is both a cause and a marker of cellular senescence [73–74]. The chromatin hallmarks of ageing—including loss of heterochromatin, altered enhancer activity and the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci—are consistent with a contribution from chronic replication stress, although direct causal links between TRCs and these chromatin changes remain to be established [66,73].

The human genetics of R-loop metabolism argues that the disease relevance of unresolved TRCs extends beyond cancer. Mutations in SETX cause ataxia with oculomotor apraxia type 2, a neurodegenerative disorder in which R-loop resolution and transcription-associated genome stability are compromised [65]; mutations in the catalytic or accessory subunits of RNase H2 cause Aicardi–Goutières syndrome, an autoinflammatory encephalopathy driven by aberrant innate immune sensing of accumulated nucleic-acid structures [75]. These examples position TRC/R-loop dysregulation as a shared vulnerability of post-mitotic and immune-competent tissues, and they strengthen the case for studying TRC-associated chromatin change in development and neurodegeneration as well as in cancer.

Development and cell-fate transitions: a window of vulnerability and opportunity

Cell-fate transitions are characterized by wholesale reprogramming of transcription: enhancer repertoires change, lineage-defining genes are activated, and the replication timing programme is remodelled [15,17]. These transitions may transiently increase TRC risk, both because new transcription programmes collide with pre-existing replication schedules and because R-loop formation accompanies the activation of many developmental genes [29,56]. The hypothesis that TRC-associated chromatin remodelling participates in stabilizing new cell identities is attractive—it would provide a mechanism by which a stress experienced during a fate transition leaves a trace on chromatin—but direct evidence is currently limited to correlative observations [15,29]. This is one of the most exciting areas for future research.

Embryonic stem cell differentiation provides a concrete illustration of the temporal coordination involved. Replication domains reorganize globally as cells exit pluripotency [15], and this reorganization is coupled to transcriptional and three-dimensional genome changes rather than being a passive consequence of gene expression [15,17]. R-loop formation accompanies the activation of many developmental genes and can shape promoter-proximal chromatin architecture during differentiation [29]. These observations define a window in which TRC burden is plausibly elevated by design: new transcription programmes engage loci whose replication schedules have not yet been remodelled, and the resolution of these conflicts may participate in the chromatin changes that stabilize the new state. Direct evidence for this scenario is currently correlative, but it makes concrete predictions that are testable with the single-cell and longitudinal approaches outlined in the Future perspectives section, for example that lineage-defining loci transiently enriched for R-loops during fate transitions should show persistent chromatin changes at those same loci after differentiation is complete.

Therapeutic opportunities

If disease states depend on a shifted TRC–chromatin equilibrium, then restoring the equilibrium—or pushing it over the edge in cancer—becomes a therapeutic strategy. In oncology, the replication stress response is already an active drug target: ATR, CHK1 and WEE1 inhibitors exploit the reliance of stressed tumour cells on checkpoint signalling, and ATR inhibition selectively kills cells with defective ATM signalling, a paradigm that has progressed into clinical development [76–78]. The ecDNA work adds a new dimension: agents that increase transcription–replication conflict (for example, by promoting R-loop formation or by targeting conflict-resolution factors) may selectively kill cells whose survival depends on a precarious conflict equilibrium [69]. Conversely, epigenetic therapies that reduce oncogenic transcription—for example BET inhibitors that suppress super-enhancer activity—may lower TRC burden and could synergize with replication-stress-based approaches [31,77]. These possibilities are testable in preclinical models and would provide a direct test of the buffering framework.

Future perspectives: technologies and questions

Mapping TRC hotspots: an integrative multi-omics strategy

A central unmet need is a genome-wide, cell-type-resolved map of TRC susceptibility that integrates all known determinants (Fig. 2). The required technologies exist: Repli-seq and OK-seq for replication timing and fork direction [16,24]; PRO-seq for engaged polymerases [27]; DRIP-seq and R-ChIP for R-loops [59,79]; ATAC-seq and CUT&Tag for chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, including at single-cell resolution [80–81]; and Hi-C for 3D organization [19]. The challenge is integration: multi-modal models trained on these features could predict conflict probability at nucleotide resolution, and single-cell replication-timing and chromatin assays could reveal how conflict risk varies across cell-cycle stage and developmental state.

Each of these assays carries limitations that constrain the interpretation of a putative hotspot map. DRIP-seq and R-ChIP differ in sensitivity, resolution and strand specificity, and both are subject to background from non-R-loop RNA–DNA hybrids [59,79]; OK-seq reports fork direction at the population level and cannot resolve direction in single forks; PRO-seq captures engaged polymerases but requires high cell numbers, although single-cell variants are emerging [27]. These caveats do not invalidate the integrative strategy, but they argue for validating predicted hotspots with at least two orthogonal readouts and for cautious interpretation of quantitative comparisons across datasets.

Testing the epigenetic memory hypothesis

The memory hypothesis requires longitudinal, lineage-resolved experiments. A feasible design is: (i) induce defined, locus-specific TRCs (for example, by inducible transcription of a reporter through a replication origin); (ii) remove the inducer; (iii) track chromatin state, transcription and replication stress across many cell divisions using CUT&Tag, RNA-seq and replication-timing assays; and (iv) test whether any persistent chromatin change influences cell behaviour, including differentiation potential [80,82]. Critical controls include distinguishing memory from ongoing low-level stress, and distinguishing cell-intrinsic epigenetic inheritance from population-level selection (Model C). The epigenetic-memory literature provides both the conceptual criteria and the technical precedents for such experiments [61–62,83].

Single-cell and spatial genomics

TRCs are intrinsically single-cell, single-locus events, yet most current data are population averages. Single-cell ATAC-seq and CUT&Tag, single-cell replication profiling and spatial transcriptomic/genomic methods now make it possible to ask whether TRC hotspots correspond to domains of coordinated chromatin remodelling in individual cells, and whether conflict-driven chromatin change is inherited by daughter cells [80–81]. These approaches will also clarify the relationship between TRC hotspots and cell-to-cell variability in gene expression, a question that is invisible to bulk assays.

Computational prediction and AI-based models

TRC susceptibility is a function of sequence, chromatin, transcription and replication features that are jointly difficult to model manually. Deep-learning models such as Enformer, which predict expression and chromatin features from DNA sequence over long ranges, provide a natural framework for learning sequence determinants of conflict-prone regions; Basenji-class models have already demonstrated that regulatory features can be predicted ab initio from sequence [84–85]. Extending such models to predict R-loop propensity, replication timing and conflict probability—and validating the predictions experimentally—is a concrete near-term goal that would give the field a quantitative, genome-wide “TRC susceptibility map”.

These models are powerful but not yet sufficient for TRC prediction. Sequence-based architectures such as Enformer and related Basenji-class models predict expression and chromatin features from DNA sequence over long ranges, but they were trained on population-averaged epigenomic data and do not explicitly represent replication timing, fork direction or the dynamic coexistence of transcription and replication in S phase [84–85]. Moreover, R-loop formation depends on co-transcriptional RNA structure and kinetics that are only partially encoded in sequence, and no existing architecture outputs conflict probability directly. A practical near-term approach is therefore to treat existing models as feature extractors: train a predictor on integrated replication-timing, transcription and R-loop tracks, and use the sequence model to generalize to loci and cell types for which those tracks are missing. This hybrid strategy would give the field a genome-wide “TRC susceptibility map” while making explicit where experimental validation is still required.

Conclusions

Transcription–replication conflicts are traditionally viewed as accidents of genome metabolism that damage DNA. The evidence reviewed here supports a more nuanced view: TRCs are spatially and temporally regulated events whose occurrence is determined by the chromatin landscape, whose burden is managed by a multi-layered buffering system, and whose resolution—or failure—can itself remodel chromatin in ways that may influence gene expression and cell fate. We have deliberately formulated the most provocative claim—that TRCs can act as epigenetic signals that create heritable memory—as a hypothesis with defined, testable criteria rather than as an established conclusion. The framework we propose makes three concrete predictions: (1) genome-wide TRC susceptibility is predictable from chromatin, replication and transcription data; (2) recurrent TRCs at defined hotspots produce persistent local chromatin changes; and (3) shifting the TRC–chromatin equilibrium is sufficient to alter cell identity, with direct implications for cancer therapy and for our understanding of ageing and development. Testing these predictions will require the integration of single-cell, longitudinal and computational approaches, but the payoff—a mechanistic link between genome stress, chromatin state and cell fate—justifies the effort.

These predictions are deliberately framed at different levels of evidence. The first is a computational goal that existing datasets can address immediately; the second is a mechanistic claim that requires the inducible-conflict systems outlined in the Future perspectives section; the third is a conceptual proposition whose strongest support would come from showing that perturbing the TRC–chromatin equilibrium changes differentiation outcomes in defined systems [29,56]. We anticipate that progress on each prediction will feed back on the framework itself, refining which elements of the three-layer model are general and which are context-specific.

Box 1 | A framework for evaluating TRC-driven epigenetic memory

We define TRC-driven epigenetic memory as a chromatin state that satisfies four criteria:

Criterion Definition Current evidence
1. Persistence The chromatin change outlasts the inducing TRC and survives cell division Partial: DDR- and R-loop-associated marks persist for hours to days; longitudinal data are lacking
2. Function The change measurably alters transcription or other chromatin-templated processes Strong: R-loops and DDR remodelling regulate local transcription and chromatin architecture
3. Self-propagation The state is inherited independently of the original trigger, via histone marks, variants or feedback loops Essentially untested for TRCs; precedents exist in other systems
4. Reversibility The state can be erased under defined conditions Experimentally addressable; unexplored for TRCs

Recommended wording for publication: “TRCs can induce persistent local chromatin remodelling that may influence transcription and cell behaviour; whether such changes constitute self-propagating epigenetic memory remains an open question.” Avoid formulations such as “TRCs establish epigenetic memory” unless criteria 3 and 4 are experimentally demonstrated.

Outstanding questions

  1. Which combination of chromatin, replication and transcription features best predicts TRC hotspots, and can this be captured in a quantitative, genome-wide model?
  2. Do pathological TRCs induced by different perturbations (oncogene activation, exogenous replication stress, R-loop dysregulation) differ in their genomic location preferences, and what determines these preferences?
  3. How are prevention and resolution factors recruited to TRC hotspots before and during conflict, and is recruitment proportional to local conflict load?
  4. Does TRC-induced chromatin remodelling persist after conflict resolution, and can it be inherited through cell division?
  5. Do recurrent TRCs at defined loci alter the buffering capacity of those loci (“adaptive” chromatin states), and is this mechanism engaged during cell-fate transitions?
  6. What is the causal contribution of TRC-associated chromatin change—versus genetic damage—to tumour evolution, senescence and ageing?
  7. Can agents that enhance transcription–replication conflict selectively kill cancer cells, particularly ecDNA-positive tumours, and can epigenetic drugs lower TRC burden in normal or premalignant tissue?
